# Close the 35-point gap before Orchestrate returns

A solo competitor who scored **65.2/100** in October 2026 (transcript 8.5/10, output 25.2/30, code 18/30, interview 13.5/30) lost **34.8 points, and 28.5 of them (82%) sit in two signals: code (12 lost) and interview (16.5 lost)**. Nothing about the next edition is confirmed as of 28 October 2026: no date, theme, or registration link exists on any official HackerRank surface, and the landing page still presents October as the latest edition ([HackerRank Orchestrate](https://www.hackerrank.com/orchestrate)). What can be prepared is the standing format, which has held across all **five editions (May, June, August, September, October 2026)**: a 24-hour solo build, four scored artifacts weighted 30/30/30/10 (code, output, interview, transcript), and a 30-minute voice interview with an AI judge that has already read the repository ([Help Center](https://help.hackerrank.com/articles/3728112689-hackerrank-orchestrate); [Behind the Scenes](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-hackerrank-orchestrate/)). Every documented podium build used one model loop wrapped in deterministic pre- and post-processing, and every Top-10 finisher in May was top-quartile on all four signals ([Behind the Scenes](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-hackerrank-orchestrate/)). The highest-leverage preparation is therefore not more research but repeated build-measure-explain practice: a defensible hybrid architecture, an independent evaluation harness whose numbers you can quote, and mock interviews drilled until every answer matches the submitted code. An independent Codex critique agrees with that direction while rejecting several of the researchers' stronger inferences; its disagreements are set out in their own section below. The reading list at the end covers every link in the research notes, grouped and marked where researchers could not fully fetch the source.

## Code and interview hold 82% of the lost points

The scoring formula has been stable from May to October: **Final = 0.10 × Chat + 0.30 × Interview + 0.30 × Output + 0.30 × Code**, each signal scored 0 to 100, with tie-breaks in the order interview, code, output, chat ([Behind the Scenes](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-hackerrank-orchestrate/); [Help Center](https://help.hackerrank.com/articles/3728112689-hackerrank-orchestrate)) **[STANDING, with the caveat "weights can vary by edition"]**. The transcript was held at 10% "because of upload errors and signs of manipulation" in May, with a promise that "future editions will iterate on this"; no change has been documented since ([Behind the Scenes](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-hackerrank-orchestrate/)). The sub-rubrics were published only for May and must be treated as carried over: code is scored on agent architecture 30%, prompt and tool craft 30%, robustness 25%, engineering rigor 15%, and "only observable source code counts. README claims, intent comments, and unused imports are ignored"; the interview on technical depth and ownership 40%, problem understanding and judgment 25%, communication clarity 20%, honesty and self-awareness 15%; the transcript on direction and architecture ownership 35%, technical specificity 25%, iteration and verification 25%, safety awareness 15%; and output per row 0 to 100 against a golden set plus one safety score, with correct labels carrying empty or generic justifications **capped at about 70** ([Behind the Scenes](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-hackerrank-orchestrate/)) **[CARRIED-OVER]**. October added problem-level criteria (deletion assessment accuracy, identity resolution, discovery of remaining data and recreation risks, policy application with evidence references, completeness and ordering of steps) without publishing percentages ([October repo](https://github.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26)).

Interview scores are the most leverageable because HackerRank has published what moves them. In May, interview score correlated with total candidate words (r = 0.615), share of longer answers (r = 0.631), and concrete explanations with trade-offs and examples (r = 0.583), while very short answers correlated negatively (r = −0.568) and the number of turns was weakly negative: "answer depth mattered more than volume" ([Behind the Scenes](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-hackerrank-orchestrate/)). Across four editions a "complete technical defence" (architecture, key decisions, test evidence, failure modes) appeared in **96%, 90%, 98% and 80% of Top-50 interviews versus 32%, 18%, 10% and 10% of Bottom-50**, the biggest gaps were "evaluation evidence and quantified results", and naming a limitation with its fallback did not weaken an answer; within each experience band a complete defence scored 2.6 to 3.6 points higher, while experience itself correlated only 0.045 to 0.125 with interview score ([rvivek X article](https://x.com/rvivek/article/2106264394414108925)). HackerRank frames the interview as its integrity signal: weaker candidates "often had similar code but couldn't defend it" ([The New Cheating Problem](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/the-new-cheating-problem-and-why-the-answer-isnt-a-stricter-test/)). Returning participants improved their interview score 58% of the time and code 57% of the time, but only 56% moved up overall, so returning helps without guaranteeing a better rank ([rvivek X article](https://x.com/rvivek/article/2106264394414108925)).

The interview itself is a 30-minute, camera-on voice session with an AI interviewer (participants call it "Chakra") that opens immediately after a successful submission and follows a fixed order: opening pitch, retrieval and architecture, safety and failure modes, implementation and code familiarity, evaluation, production monitoring, what is novel, plus how you worked with AI tools ([Help Center](https://help.hackerrank.com/articles/3728112689-hackerrank-orchestrate); [October page](https://www.hackerrank.com/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26); [Behind the Scenes](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-hackerrank-orchestrate/)). In May the window stayed open for four hours after the hackathon ended ([May README](https://github.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-may26/blob/main/README.md)) **[CARRIED-OVER]**. The judge has read the repository first: a landing-page testimonial reports "An AI judge read my repo and found my dead code", and an August participant says it "had read the full write-up beforehand" ([October page](https://www.hackerrank.com/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26); [Houssam Substack](https://h0ussamcl4p.substack.com/p/i-fired-my-ai-from-one-job-39-54)). The questions are exact-value questions. A September participant ranked 139th was asked which value estimated variable expenses (the code used the median of up to five recent amounts), what happens first when salary and bills land on the same day (the code credited income first; the author believed the opposite), how correctness was verified across all 250 requests when only 25 had public answers, and "what did you design yourself?", and "bombed several" because the questions were "more specific than the author's prep" ([dev.to](https://dev.to/earlgreyhot1701d/my-first-ai-judge-interview-what-could-possibly-go-wrong-22el)). The official answer shape is "start with the direct answer, add one concrete detail, tie it back to the task, and then stop", and it is explicitly fine to say AI wrote parts of the code as long as you can explain what you submitted and why ([Getting Better at Orchestrate](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/getting-better-at-orchestrate/)).

## Submission format tightened every edition; build the tooling before the clock starts

The help center's standing list is three uploads, each up to 50 MB, via the link in the challenge email: a code ZIP (source plus a README with setup and run instructions, secrets from environment variables, deterministic where possible, no venvs, build artifacts or provided data), an output file in the problem's format, and the AI chat transcript; the interview starts after a successful submission ([Help Center](https://help.hackerrank.com/articles/3728112689-hackerrank-orchestrate)) **[STANDING]**. The per-edition repos have added requirements on top, and the trend is strictly toward more auditable process artifacts.

| Requirement | May 2026 | June 2026 | October 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Output | predictions CSV, 29 tickets | `output.csv`, one row per claim, ten exact columns, `none` for empty, semicolon-separated multi-values | `output.csv`, 518 rows, header `case_id,deletion_status,steps_json`, sorted by `case_id` |
| Transcript | `$HOME/hackerrank_orchestrate/log.txt` | same path; per-tool dividers if several tools | root `log.txt`, append-only, structured per-turn entries with exact `tool=` and `model=` |

Sources: [May README](https://github.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-may26/blob/main/README.md); [June repo](https://github.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-june26); [October repo](https://github.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26); [October AGENTS.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26/main/AGENTS.md); [October agent_trace_instructions.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26/main/agent_trace_instructions.md) **[CARRIED-OVER]**.

October's rules are the strictest and the best guide to what comes next. The `code.zip` had to exclude `dataset/`, `.venv`/`venv`, `node_modules`, `__pycache__` and nested archives; the `evaluation_report.md` had to cover case, row, field and sequence accuracy, at least three custom test cases, failures found and improvements made; the `usage_report.md` had to cover the final full-dataset run with providers, model names, call counts, input and output tokens, per-request tokens and estimated cost; and "Metrics must be in Markdown. Automated judges ignore .csv, .json, .jsonl, .sql, and .log files" ([October repo](https://github.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26)). The `log.txt` needed a session-start entry (timestamp, tool, model, repo root, branch, worktree, parent agent, time remaining) and a per-turn entry after every user message with the verbatim prompt (secrets redacted), a two-to-five-sentence response summary, actions and a context block; `tool=` and `model=` had to be exact, not placeholders ([October AGENTS.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26/main/AGENTS.md)). The `agent-trace.zip` required the complete raw trace of every session, one subfolder per tool, with "do not convert JSONL, SQLite, or other structured logs into screenshots or summaries" and auth files removed: Claude Code session JSONL from `~/.claude/projects/` plus `/export` output, Codex `rollout-*.jsonl` from `~/.codex/sessions/` (never `auth.json`), Antigravity conversation folders, OpenCode session exports ([October agent_trace_instructions.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26/main/agent_trace_instructions.md)). Hard constraints: run from the terminal (`python3 code/main.py` starter), read `dataset/`, write root `output.csv` with exactly 518 rows, deterministic, use only evidence at or before each case's `audit_at`, work on macOS, Linux and Windows, and "the October deadline is not configured in the repo. Don't invent one" ([October AGENTS.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26/main/AGENTS.md)). Whether `agent-trace.zip` is now a standing requirement is unknown; the help center still lists three uploads. A participant opened an issue asking whether an exported ChatGPT transcript was acceptable, which shows the transcript rule assumes a local coding agent logging to `log.txt` ([Issue #22](https://github.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26/issues/22)).

Because the rules changed every edition (May's output lived in `support_tickets/`, October's at root with two mandatory reports), a packaging script from a previous edition cannot be reused blindly; re-read the edition README on the day. What can be prepared in advance is the machinery: a `log.txt` emitter that satisfies the October AGENTS.md shape, a trace-collection script per harness, a packaging check that validates output rows, columns and allowed values, excludes forbidden directories, scans for secrets, and records artifact hashes. October participants built exactly this: one PR redacts credentials from the transcript, excludes unrelated sessions, keeps the trace archive separate from the source package and records hashes ([vatsalyd PR #9](https://github.com/vatsalyd/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26/pull/9)); another notes that re-running the repackage command produces a ZIP with a different hash, so the committed ZIP is kept as the historical artifact ([Aditya-Ranjan1234 repo](https://github.com/Aditya-Ranjan1234/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26)); the community orchestrate-kit ships a `package_submission.py` that checks output, README, transcript and secrets ([orchestrate-kit](https://github.com/NITISH-R-G/hackerrank-orchestrate-skills)) **[UNVERIFIED, community tool]**. HackerRank's own pre-submission checklist is to review the CSV "as if you've never seen the project": all rows present, no missing or duplicate IDs, exact columns, labels in the allowed set, similar cases treated similarly, justifications that cite evidence and surface uncertainty; and to purge hardcoded paths, embedded secrets, undocumented manual steps, generated files mixed into source, and names like "helper, final, utils, test2" ([Getting Better at Orchestrate](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/getting-better-at-orchestrate/)).

## Winners wrap one model loop in deterministic code and can quote every threshold

The three best-documented podium builds converge on one architecture. May's winner Saai Syvendra built a single agent with a single read-only corpus tool exposing three functions (semantic search, full-file read, exact grep), hybrid BM25 plus vector retrieval with rank fusion and reranking, a multilingual prompt-injection check that refuses suspicious tickets before any model call, cheap rules for pleasantries and empty tickets, Haiku for product-area tagging, and citation enforcement in which a citation counts only if the file was opened that turn and uncited answers become escalations; his advice is "Reach for a multi-agent system only when a dire need forces you to" ([Saai Syvendra: How I Won](https://saai.syvendra.com/myspace/hackerrank-orchestrate); [code](https://github.com/saai-syvendra/hackerrank-orchestrate)). June's third place, Swayam Mishra, used a three-stage PREP (code), LOOK (model describes), DECIDE (code) pipeline, "the model describes, code decides", with a single typed object as the only seam, pure decision functions with 100+ tests, history fields structurally excluded from the decision record, and cached observations re-graded offline at roughly $0.075 to $0.20 per claim; his earlier May entry was a 15-stage pipeline that "finished mid-pack" because he "could not clearly explain or measure what each stage contributed" ([Swayam Mishra](https://swayam-mishra.medium.com/how-to-build-ai-agents-you-can-actually-trust-d0c37289f48d); [code](https://github.com/swayam-mishra/hackerrank-orchestrate)). August's second place, Caleb Andersen, ran one agent per message in a bounded tool loop with deterministic code on both sides: a pre-model "dossier" of per-recipient and per-sender behaviour rates (zero denominators become null, not 0.0), three-axis model scoring resolved in a fixed order with risk as an absolute veto, a validator that returns rejections to the model rather than defaulting, a seven-rule no-LLM safety gate that can only move toward mute, and a tool enum limited to the row's evidence shortlist so invented IDs cannot be emitted; 161 model calls per 110-row run ([Caleb Andersen case study](https://calebandersen.dev/work/orchestrate-router); [code](https://github.com/caleb-andersen/hackerrank-orchestrate-august26)). August's winner, Mrinal Desai, states the same principle in his testimonial, "Let the model describe; let deterministic code decide", though no repo or write-up by him was found ([October page](https://www.hackerrank.com/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26)). No write-up, repo or interview was found for the June, September or provisional October winners; the June winner's Medium post was blocked by Medium's bot check and must be read in a browser.

HackerRank's own data backs the pattern. In May, single agents with tools or RAG took the best rank (1), 7 of the Top 10 and 27 of the Top 50; graph/state-machine designs peaked at rank 2; multi-stage or multi-agent pipelines at rank 3; deterministic rule pipelines at rank 17 with one in the Top 50; trained ML classifiers at rank 46; and single-prompt agents at rank 77 with none in the Top 50 ([Behind the Scenes](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-hackerrank-orchestrate/)). Across four editions the Top-50 mix was **46.5% single agent with tools/RAG, 35.0% model-assisted pipeline, 14.0% multi-agent and 4.5% direct single agent**; all 200 Top-50 submissions used structured output, guardrails or validation; submissions showing five or six engineering-loop stages averaged 42.12 on non-transcript evaluations versus 27.38 for two or fewer; explicit evaluation pipelines appeared in 98.9% of the top quarter versus 68.6% of the bottom quarter; purely deterministic pipelines "clustered in the bottom quarter"; and "no single model, harness, or architecture determined success" ([rvivek X article](https://x.com/rvivek/article/2106264394414108925)). Of the 200 Top-50 slots, 149 were top-quarter on all four metrics, which is the "balanced builder" profile the May post-mortem identified ([rvivek X article](https://x.com/rvivek/article/2106264394414108925); [Behind the Scenes](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-hackerrank-orchestrate/)).

October's problem complicates the "penalised determinism" reading. Its scoring is sequence- and structure-driven (ordered steps, consecutive numbering, valid evidence IDs), the provisional top three all scored 28.5 to 29.4 on output, far above any earlier edition's best output score of 24.0, and several public October repos advertise a fully deterministic auditor with "Runtime LLM calls: zero" ([October leaderboard](https://www.hackerrank.com/contests/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26/challenges/ghost-data-still-exists/leaderboard); [Sharv619 repo](https://github.com/Sharv619/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26); [LangerSword repo](https://github.com/LangerSword/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26)) **[UNVERIFIED, self-reported]**. The past-editions researcher inferred that those zero-LLM repos leave code-ZIP points on the table; the Codex critique disputes that inference (see the Second opinion section). What is not in dispute is that the code rubric's largest line item, architecture at 30%, asks whether the project is "a real agent with tool-calling loops, or just a hardcoded workflow with LLM calls", and that a fixed pipeline cannot answer that question well in the interview ([Behind the Scenes](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-hackerrank-orchestrate/)).

Tool choice correlates with rank but HackerRank insists process drives it. In May, Antigravity was the most-used harness (33.7%) yet "Not a single Top 10 submission came from an Antigravity user"; Claude Code was 14.4% overall but 44% of the Top 50 and 7 of the Top 10 ([Behind the Scenes](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-hackerrank-orchestrate/); [What 12,885 Developers Taught Us](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/what-12885-developers-taught-us-about-building-with-ai/)). Across four editions Claude Code was the most common Top-50 harness every time, Antigravity's share falls from 39% among students to 11% among seven-plus-year developers while Claude Code's rises from 14% to 41%, Gemini was the most-identified model family from June onward, and the recurring role split was Gemini for multimodal extraction, GPT for verification, Claude for generation and review, and Llama or Qwen as low-cost local models, with the caveat "Process mattered more than tool choice" ([rvivek X article](https://x.com/rvivek/article/2106264394414108925)). The practical reading is that the harness that writes the cleanest AGENTS.md-driven `log.txt` and the raw trace you can export is the right one, and that experienced builders' preference for Claude Code is a signal about transcript hygiene rather than a causal lever.

The interview preparation of the one participant with a documented three-edition climb is the most concrete template available. Caleb Andersen went from 70th in May to 5th in June to 2nd in August, with interview scores of 15.6, 20.4 and 26.1; he wrote interview material during the build (each threshold logged with its value, where it is defined, where it is read, what happens when it is hit and why), practised against a timer with the answer shape "direct answer with the exact value and file, one concrete detail, tie back to the task, then stop", spent most of the 24 hours on planning (reading data, feature spec, architecture), froze code two hours before the deadline for packaging and interview prep, and treated the judges' written feedback as a specification, addressing all four June items in August ([Caleb Andersen case study](https://calebandersen.dev/work/orchestrate-router)). Whether the preparation caused the improvement is a separate question the Codex critique raises; the method is still the only documented one that coincided with an 10.5-point interview gain. The inverse case is equally instructive: Swayam Mishra had the second-best code in June (27.6/30) but an interview score of 21.3 that ranked 120th; his advice is to lead with failure modes, discuss trade-offs rather than features, and keep a decisions log ([Swayam Mishra](https://swayam-mishra.medium.com/how-to-build-ai-agents-you-can-actually-trust-d0c37289f48d)).

## Build the loop, the evals and the budget before the clock starts

The agent-building research resolves the "agent versus workflow" question that the code rubric and the interview both probe. Anthropic's definition is that "Workflows are systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths. Agents, on the other hand, are systems where LLMs dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage", with both grouped as agentic systems; its three principles are simplicity, transparency "by explicitly showing the agent's planning steps", and careful tool interface design, and its advice is to "start by using LLM APIs directly" because frameworks "can obscure the underlying prompts and responses" ([Anthropic, Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-effective-agents)). OpenAI draws the same line: applications that integrate LLMs "but don't use them to control workflow execution" are not agents; an agent "recognizes when a workflow is complete and can proactively correct its actions" and "dynamically selects the appropriate tools depending on the workflow's current state", and the recommendation is to "maximize a single agent's capabilities first" ([OpenAI, A practical guide to building agents](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf)). The hybrid both guides endorse is deterministic loading, validation and output-writing around a bounded investigation loop in which the model chooses which evidence tool to call next, and the trace should show tool results visibly changing the next call and a model-initiated stop; a fixed parse-summarise-emit pipeline shows the same call sequence every run, which is the tell for "workflow" **[UNVERIFIED: researcher inference]**.

The minimal robust loop is well documented and should be written and tested before the edition. With the Anthropic API, a `tool_use` stop reason means run every requested tool and return all `tool_result` blocks in one user message (errors as `is_error: true`), then repeat until `end_turn`, a validated structured output, a max-turns, token or wall-clock budget, or a guardrail; `strict: true` on tool definitions makes calls "always match your schema exactly", and `disable_parallel_tool_use` forces one call per turn for a strictly serial, readable trace ([Anthropic tool use overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/overview.md)). OpenAI's Agents SDK encodes the same exits: output is final when the model produces typed text with no tool calls, exceeding `max_turns` raises `MaxTurnsExceeded`, guardrail tripwires abort runs, and a structured output that fails validation raises `ModelBehaviorError` unless a fallback handler exists ([OpenAI Agents SDK, Running agents](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/running_agents/)). Pydantic AI validates tool arguments before your code runs and returns validation errors to the model for retry, guarantees the typed `output_type`, supports dependency injection for test doubles and is OpenTelemetry-native ([Pydantic AI overview](https://pydantic.dev/docs/ai/overview/)); smolagents fits in about a thousand lines but its `CodeAgent` executes model-written Python and needs a sandbox ([smolagents docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/smolagents/index)). The researcher's ranking (raw SDK loop or tool runner lowest risk, Pydantic AI next, OpenAI Agents SDK a poorer fit when the model is Claude, LangGraph highest learning-curve risk) is an inference, and its LangGraph characterisation rests only on 2026 comparison blogs ([AWS Builder](https://builder.aws.com/content/3AzsgG6TreTO3uLRqpWNxfEyUhe/picking-an-ai-agent-framework-in-2026); [Langfuse comparison](https://langfuse.com/blog/2025-03-19-ai-agent-comparison)) **[UNVERIFIED]**. Several current-API constraints that break naive loops (forced `tool_choice` returning HTTP 400 on the newest models, structured output via `output_config.format`, append-only thinking blocks, visible task budgets) come from a locally cached skill reference dated 6 October 2026 and need a live smoke test against the models overview before adoption ([models overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/overview.md)) **[UNVERIFIED]**.

Tracing for the judge is a solved problem only if you own the emitter. No tool surveyed natively produces a judge-ready Markdown transcript, OpenTelemetry's GenAI semantic conventions were still marked "Development" with no tagged release as of mid-2026 ([Dash0](https://www.dash0.com/knowledge/opentelemetry-genai-semantic-conventions-explained)) **[UNVERIFIED, secondary]**, and October's rule that automated judges ignore `.json` and `.log` files means any trace evidence must be summarised into the Markdown evaluation report anyway ([October repo](https://github.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26)). The defensible design is a custom JSONL event log recording each round-trip's plan, tool name and arguments, truncated result, `usage` and `stop_reason`, rendered to Markdown by a short script, optionally mirrored to Logfire or a self-hosted Langfuse for a visual span tree; Anthropic's evals guidance to "read the transcripts" and to record runtime, tool calls, tokens and errors per run applies directly ([Anthropic, Demystifying evals](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents); [Writing tools for agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/writing-tools-for-agents)). Remember the scored transcript is the conversation with the coding tool, "not the runtime agent's logs or reasoning trace" ([June repo](https://github.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-june26)); runtime tracing serves the build, the evaluation report and the interview, not the 10% transcript signal.

Evaluation is where mid-table entries lose both output and interview points, and the method is cheap. Anthropic's roadmap is to start with 20 to 50 tasks drawn from real failures, prefer deterministic code graders ("fast, cheap, objective, reproducible, but brittle"), use model graders only for the nuanced residue after human calibration, build balanced sets that test when a behaviour should and should not occur, report pass^k when consistency matters (75% per trial over three trials gives about 42%), and validate improvements on held-out sets to avoid overfitting ([Anthropic, Demystifying evals](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents); [Writing tools for agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/writing-tools-for-agents)). Hamel Husain's judge method is binary pass/fail with written critiques, one failure mode per judge, about 30 examples to discover failure modes and about 100 per failure mode to validate a judge, with true-positive and true-negative rates measured separately because a rare-failure judge that always passes scores high raw agreement ([Hamel Husain, LLM-as-a-Judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)). Known judge biases (position, verbosity, self-enhancement) are documented ([ICLR 2025](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2025/poster/31088)). The Orchestrate-specific lesson is to grade every scored column, not the headline: Caleb Andersen pushed routing to 90% while "the other output columns lagged" and concluded "the measurement was the weak point, not the system" ([Caleb Andersen](https://calebandersen.dev/work/orchestrate-router)); the September participant validated format and balances but never checked the 25 public expected answers ([dev.to](https://dev.to/earlgreyhot1701d/my-first-ai-judge-interview-what-could-possibly-go-wrong-22el)); and an October repo honestly reports 32/32 statuses but 12/32 exact step arrays on a sample that "was reused heavily during development and is not a held-out benchmark" ([Aditya-Ranjan1234 repo](https://github.com/Aditya-Ranjan1234/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26)). One caution: Anthropic's "Grade what the agent produced, not the path it took" refers to its own agent evals; when the edition's contract prescribes step order, as October's did, the contract wins (the Codex critique makes this point and it is correct).

Every edition has included prompt injection or scams in the data, and the defences are architectural. The six published patterns (action-selector, plan-then-execute, LLM map-reduce, dual LLM, code-then-execute, context minimisation) share one principle: once untrusted input is ingested, "it is impossible for that input to trigger any consequential actions" ([Simon Willison, design patterns](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jun/13/prompt-injection-design-patterns/); [arXiv 2506.08837](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08837)). OpenAI's layered guardrail taxonomy (relevance and safety classifiers, PII filters, tool risk ratings, rules-based limits, output validation) and Anthropic's context-engineering advice (token-efficient tools, just-in-time retrieval, bounded responses, structured note-taking) map directly onto an evidence-reading agent ([OpenAI guide](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf); [Anthropic, context engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)). For Orchestrate the practical measures are read-only evidence tools and a single output writer with no network, each document framed as untrusted data with a provenance tag, bounded tool responses and steps, every finding required to cite an evidence locator that a deterministic post-check verifies exists, and a canary injection in the eval set. The strongest Orchestrate-specific evidence is that constraining the output space beats prompting: one August participant lifted evidence-ID accuracy from 39% to 54% by replacing model selection with deterministic Jaccard overlap over retrieved candidates, concluding "Constraining the output space eliminates a failure mode. Prompting and validation only reduce it" ([Houssam Substack](https://h0ussamcl4p.substack.com/p/i-fired-my-ai-from-one-job-39-54)), and Caleb's tool enum limited to the row's shortlist made invented IDs impossible to emit ([Caleb Andersen](https://calebandersen.dev/work/orchestrate-router)). The researcher's stronger claims (that a citation post-check "doubles as an injection check", that a plan-then-execute preamble shows the plan was formed before untrusted text was read) are inferences the Codex critique rejects as overstated; see below.

Budgeting has to be done per full-dataset run, because the usage report demands it. The Anthropic pricing page as of 28 October 2026 lists Haiku 5.5 at $0.10/$0.50 per million input/output tokens (prompts up to 100K), Sonnet 5.5 at $2/$10, Opus 5.5 at $4/$20 and Fable 5.1 at $10/$50, with cache reads at $0.01 to $0.25 and a 50% Batch API discount ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing.md)). The researcher's sketch for a 20-turn investigation loop with about 30K cached context per turn is roughly **$0.35 per case on Sonnet 5.5, $0.70 on Opus 5.5 and $0.03 on Haiku 5.5**; at October's 518 cases that is about **$181 per full Sonnet run** before retries and repeated evaluation sweeps, not "cents per run", as the Codex critique corrects. Prompt caching matches the prefix in the order tools, system, messages, any byte change invalidates everything after it, and `usage.cache_read_input_tokens` staying at zero across repeated calls means a silent invalidator such as a timestamp in the system prompt; caches are model-scoped, so a multi-model ladder forfeits reuse ([Anthropic prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching.md)) **[UNVERIFIED: page not fetched this session; mechanics from a local cached reference]**. A reproducibility warning from the October landing page belongs in the usage report's limitations: one participant found that temperature 0.0 did not make his pipeline reproducible, with 43 of 140 readings differing ([October page](https://www.hackerrank.com/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26)).

## Community pitfalls repeat: closed-set choices, stale explanations, dirty transcripts

The official pitfall list is specific and worth internalising verbatim: hidden architecture (everything in one prompt), unclear naming, hardcoded paths and secrets, over-abstraction, writing model labels to output without checking the allowed set, over-answering when evidence is missing, one bad row breaking the whole run, not fencing injected instructions, vague AI requests ("build the solution"), and uncritical acceptance of over-complicated AI designs ([Getting Better at Orchestrate](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/getting-better-at-orchestrate/)). The May post-mortem adds the interview and transcript failures: "We used RAG" without explaining the retrieval choice; "validated" or "safety" without concrete failures; contradicting the code; tool narration ("Claude created the pipeline") instead of ownership language ("I chose", "I rejected"); README or interview claims of features the code does not implement; and transcripts that were raw output logs where participants asked for answers directly, which is why only 658 of 1,349 May transcripts were "clean and usable" ([Behind the Scenes](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-hackerrank-orchestrate/); [What 12,885 Developers Taught Us](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/what-12885-developers-taught-us-about-building-with-ai/)).

The participant-reported failures cluster into four mechanisms. First, the model choosing from a closed set (labels, evidence IDs, step order) without a code-side constraint, which was the dominant output-point loss in August and is the dominant risk in a sequence-scored problem like October's ([Houssam Substack](https://h0ussamcl4p.substack.com/p/i-fired-my-ai-from-one-job-39-54); [Caleb Andersen](https://calebandersen.dev/work/orchestrate-router)). Second, stale or wrong self-knowledge: believing the code debits before it credits, or a validator that passed because it reused the same wrong rent value as the forecast (a 12% rent increase became €12) ([dev.to](https://dev.to/earlgreyhot1701d/my-first-ai-judge-interview-what-could-possibly-go-wrong-22el)). Third, over-building and noise-chasing: the 15-stage pipeline that finished mid-pack, an AI-driven review that produced 24 candidate bugs of which 8 were real and 3 worth fixing, and chasing run-to-run noise instead of measuring variance ([Swayam Mishra](https://swayam-mishra.medium.com/how-to-build-ai-agents-you-can-actually-trust-d0c37289f48d)); a related wrong-theory debugging episode cost one August participant about two hours because he assumed scam over-prediction when his harness showed over-citation ([Houssam Substack](https://h0ussamcl4p.substack.com/p/i-fired-my-ai-from-one-job-39-54)). Fourth, data-shape assumptions: a May entrant's code assumed a `ticket` column where the data had `Issue`, `Subject` and `Company` ([Akshay J K, Medium](https://medium.com/@akshayjk0701/building-an-ai-support-triage-agent-in-24-hours-my-hackerrank-orchestrate-2026-experience-b635f436b459)) **[UNVERIFIED: Medium 403, read via proxy]**. Time management is the fifth, less visible, failure: the interview opens immediately after submission and the May window lasted four hours, so the last one to two hours of the build belong to packaging and interview preparation, not features ([October page](https://www.hackerrank.com/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26); [May README](https://github.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-may26/blob/main/README.md)). A May entrant who built an LLM-free pipeline could not complete the interview at all for personal reasons, a reminder that the interview slot is unforgiving and reschedules are not guaranteed ([Akshay J K, Medium](https://medium.com/@akshayjk0701/building-an-ai-support-triage-agent-in-24-hours-my-hackerrank-orchestrate-2026-experience-b635f436b459); [Help Center](https://help.hackerrank.com/articles/3728112689-hackerrank-orchestrate)).

The only official community is the Orchestrate Discord; no Reddit, Hacker News or YouTube discussion was found, public blogs lag results by one to several weeks, and general questions go to Discord while account and deadline issues go to help@hackerrank.com with the edition name, username and registration email ([Help Center](https://help.hackerrank.com/articles/3728112689-hackerrank-orchestrate); [Discord invite](https://discord.com/invite/hackerrank-orchestrate)). The unofficial orchestrate-kit offers an interview simulator with four judge personas (Architect, Skeptic, Security Reviewer, Practitioner) and cross-examination such as "was that figure measured or recalled?", but its author states the scorer is uncalibrated because no official grader is published ([orchestrate-kit](https://github.com/NITISH-R-G/hackerrank-orchestrate-skills)) **[UNVERIFIED]**. General AI-agent hackathon rubrics (Microsoft, Cloudflare/NVIDIA, MIT CSAIL, GitLab, AI Tinkerers) weight working code, genuine multi-step tool use, impact and demo clarity, and organiser playbooks now push "score the trace logs" over "videos and vibes" ([Microsoft rules](https://microsoft.github.io/AI_Agents_Hackathon/rules/); [AngelHack playbook](https://angelhack.com/blog/ai-agent-hackathon/)). What transfers to Orchestrate is the real loop, the quantified harness and visible guardrails; what does not is pitch polish, because Orchestrate has no human panel or video, only the 30-minute cross-examination, and the Codex critique rightly rates these general playbooks as low-value for further reading.

## Second opinion (Codex)

An independent critique from OpenAI Codex read the same notes and agrees with their overall direction, "simple hybrid architecture, explicit evaluation, and technical ownership", while reweighting the priority and rejecting several inferences. Its headline matches this report's: code plus interview account for 28.5 of the 34.8 lost points, 82% of the gap, and reaching 24/30 in both with output and transcript held constant would produce 81.7, "an illustrative target, not a forecast". It then disagrees on seven points, and the disagreements are presented here as conflicts rather than averaged.

On determinism, the past-editions and community notes infer that purely deterministic pipelines are penalised on the code signal and that October's zero-LLM repos leave code points on the table. Codex says this "overstates the evidence": the May rubric does reward agentic architecture, so the concern is real, but "architecture rankings do not establish why your October code earned 18/30; May's deterministic category even reached rank 17" ([Behind the Scenes](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-hackerrank-orchestrate/)). Its prescription is to add model-directed investigation only where it resolves uncertainty and to measure its contribution by ablation. The conflict is direct: the researchers treat "add agency" as a general lever; Codex treats it as a hypothesis to test on this specific codebase. The researchers' view is better supported by HackerRank's aggregate data (bottom-quarter clustering across four editions); Codex's view is better supported for the individual case, because nothing in the notes explains the 18/30.

On winner imitation, the community note infers that Caleb Andersen's interview rose 10.5 points "purely via prep process" and that Claude Code usage and longer answers are levers. Codex calls this "weak causal evidence": Claude usage, longer answers and one participant's improvement "do not prove that switching tools, speaking longer, or following his preparation caused better scores", and the word "purely" is unjustified. This report has adopted Codex's framing: the methods are documented templates, not proven causes.

On security, the tooling note claims a deterministic citation post-check doubles as an injection check, that a plan-then-execute preamble shows the plan preceded untrusted text, and that one resisted injection demonstrates robustness. Codex rejects all three as "too strong": "A valid citation ID can support a false claim; opening a file does not establish entailment. A planning preamble does not enforce control-flow integrity. One resisted injection establishes one passing test." The views conflict and Codex's is the more rigorous; the researcher's measures remain worth implementing but should be described in the interview as partial mitigations with named residual risks.

On tooling, the tooling note ranks a raw SDK loop as lowest risk and states that no tracing tool exports Markdown or HTML. Codex says the ranking is "too confident" because risk "depends on your familiarity and handling of retries, tool errors, termination, and state", and the export claim is "unsupported when the relevant documentation was not checked" (the note itself concedes Langfuse and Braintrust docs were not checked). On budgeting, Codex points out that the tooling note's "cents per run" hides scale: its own $0.35-per-case estimate is $181.30 for 518 cases before retries and repeated evaluation, so "cents per run" needs a clearly defined unit. Both corrections are accepted in this report.

On evaluation, Codex flags that the tooling note's generic advice against rigid ordering checks "can conflict with the contract": October "explicitly prescribed phase, operation, system, and resource order", so "follow the task's required serialization" ([October problem statement](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/interviewstreet/hackerrank-orchestrate-october26/main/problem_statement.md)). This is a genuine conflict between Anthropic's general evals guidance and the edition's rules, and the edition's rules win. On research breadth, Codex argues that framework surveys, other hackathons' pitch criteria, provisional rankings and model catalogues "now have lower value than repeated build-measure-explain practice", and that "cached API claims need a live smoke test before adoption"; the researchers' notes are broader than Codex thinks useful, and this report's reading list marks those categories accordingly.

Codex also lists what the notes miss, and these are the most actionable additions. A personal failure diagnosis: reconstruct the October interview questions and classify each weak answer as missing knowledge, faulty design, stale recollection, weak evidence or unclear delivery, because each needs a different remedy. An explicit unresolved-evidence state: the competitor's earlier retrospective flagged missing evidence becoming `COMPLETE`, so practise distinguishing proven, contradicted and unresolved internally before mapping to the permitted output contract. Independent tests that can falsify the implementation: rename identifiers consistently, shuffle input rows, insert irrelevant records, remove decisive evidence, introduce conflicting timestamps, and define the expected effect before running. Cache correctness and cold execution: test unseen cases with empty caches, invalidate on changes to evidence, prompts, model configuration or schemas, and distinguish replay reproducibility from fresh-inference consistency. A submission evidence chain tying ZIP, output, reports, configuration and the final run together with hashes and a run identifier, so "three different artifacts should not describe three different versions". A realistic solo rehearsal including rate limits, interrupted execution, clean installation, full-data runtime, upload time, sleep and a voice interview, because "a universal 'freeze two hours before' rule is inadequate if regeneration takes longer."

Codex's ranked top ten actions, in its order: record three repository-specific mock interviews and repeat failed questions until answers match the code; manually trace five difficult cases end to end; build an independent evaluation harness with an untouched test subset; prototype one bounded investigation loop against the baseline and measure it by ablation; require explain-back (mechanism, alternative, counterexample, limitation) before accepting consequential AI changes; run missing-evidence, contradiction, injection and tool-failure tests; exercise empty-cache and changed-input runs; measure full-run cost, throughput and recovery and choose a primary model plus tested fallback; rehearse ZIP extraction, execution and artifact reconciliation; complete one timed challenge in a different domain. Its interview drill uses questions that force predictions and evidence: trace a row from input to output and name the function that calls the model; show two cases where an observation changed the next tool call; what you conclude when the dashboard says complete but a source is missing; what happens at the retry limit, on malformed JSON, or when the fallback model refuses; what your accuracy number's denominator is and which cases influenced development; what invalidates a cached answer; what measured benefit disappears if you remove the model ladder or reranker; which AI suggestion you rejected and which you accepted without enough verification; how the system resumes mid-run without duplicating or losing results. Its answer protocol is direct answer, actual mechanism and file, concrete case or measured result, limitation and trade-off, stop, in 45 to 90 seconds, with figures labelled measured or estimated and no invented thresholds or unimplemented features; after each mock, check every factual assertion against the submitted code and score correctness, specificity, evidence and consistency rather than eloquence, which matches HackerRank's own guidance ([Getting Better at Orchestrate](https://www.hackerrank.com/blog/getting-better-at-orchestrate/)).

## Conclusion

The research changes the preparation question from "what will the next edition be?" to "what does a 65.2 look like from the judge's side?" The answer is an output file that is already competitive, a transcript that is near its ceiling, and a code-and-interview pair that reads as a system the builder cannot fully account for. HackerRank's own numbers say that gap is the integrity signal it designed the format to detect, and that it closes through process rather than tooling or experience: a complete technical defence was present in 80 to 98% of Top-50 interviews regardless of years worked. The five editions also show the organisers steadily converting soft signals into hard artifacts (named Markdown reports, exact `tool=` and `model=` fields, raw harness traces), so the next edition will almost certainly reward a competitor who arrives with the logging, packaging, evaluation and cost-reporting machinery already built and rehearsed on a different domain.

The one place the research and the second opinion genuinely diverge, whether the 18/30 code score came from too little agency or from something only an ablation on this codebase can reveal, is itself the right first task: prototype one bounded investigation loop against the current deterministic baseline, measure what it changes on a held-out subset, and be ready to say in the interview exactly what measured benefit disappears when it is removed. Everything else in the preparation plan, from the mock interviews to the evidence chain, exists to make that sentence, and a hundred like it, true and sayable in 45 seconds.
